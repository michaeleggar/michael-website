# Writing audit

A deep pass on the words across michael-eggar.com, measured against how Smith & Diction write about their own work. Nothing in the site was changed; every rewrite below is ready to paste, and anything I could not verify is marked `[like this]` and listed again in the questions at the end.

A note on sources. Medium refuses automated requests, so I read Smith & Diction through the mirror of their case studies on Substack (Calendly, Cowboy Space Corp, Perplexity, 41 West, Living Roots, Anterior, and their process post) plus their work page and search excerpts of the older Medium posts (BOK, Exposure, Elixr, Sabbatical, Suburban Enterprises, Paradigm). That is seven full write-ups and six partial ones, enough to be confident about the voice.

---

## 1. What Smith & Diction do well

Their write-ups run 800 to 4,000 words with punny section headings. Michael's case studies are a lede and a paragraph or two, on purpose. So the question is not "write like them" but "which of their habits survive at 120 words". These do.

1. **They open on a moment, not a definition.** An email arriving, a first meeting, their own history with the product. The client's industry comes second, inside the story. *On this site:* five of six decks open "A website for…" or "The website for…". Open on the client's situation instead, and let "website" be implied by the page.

2. **The brief is stated as a tension or in the client's own words.** Perplexity wanted a brand like a Scandinavian subway system. Anterior had to feel reliable and futuristic at once. 41 West needed funky, but still fine in a corporate slide deck. *On this site:* most pages never say what the client wanted or what was hard. Golden Mesa (sell a weekend from four hours away, promotions change weekly) is the only deck with a real tension in it. Give every page one.

3. **A short sentence after a long one.** They end runs of chatty clauses with a fragment that lands. One of theirs, in full: "Most of it was killed." *On this site:* the decks are single 30 to 38-word sentences with participial tails ("…built to sell a weekend… and keep up with…", "…organizing a growing library…", "…explaining 1.3 square miles… and getting them into…"). Break each into two or three sentences and let one be short.

4. **They admit the hard part.** "the hardest part of this whole project was the symbol" (Calendly). Living Roots confesses the labels weren't the first pass. Cowboy Space Corp counts the symbol versions (upwards of 20). The admission is what makes the rest believable. *On this site:* nothing was ever difficult. The chat app comes closest ("grew fast under production pressure", "after the first shoot day I added…") and it is the best-written page because of it. Every other page needs one honest sentence.

5. **They explain the why of one decision, tied to a behaviour.** Not a feature list; one choice, and the person it was for. *On this site:* bodies are inventories. "I built custom post types for services, work, team members, and job openings, and a work archive that filters by service and industry" is a changelog. Pick the one decision a visitor can feel and say why.

6. **Specific nouns, no metrics.** They name typefaces, tools, colours as words (burnt orange, dusty black), collaborators by first name. They never report conversion, revenue, or outcomes, and never quote a client testimonial. *On this site:* good instinct already (navy and gold, Play/Eat/Stay, hot seat drawing, "the roster"). Keep that and add people. The ledger names the contributors; the prose never does.

7. **"We", "I", and "you", freely.** They talk to the reader. *On this site:* the Golden Mesa and OKC Innovation District bodies contain no first person at all, and those are the two biggest projects. Michael is the only person on this site; the prose should sound like him in the room.

8. **Humour is an aside, never the point.** One wink per piece, usually self-deprecating, then back to work. *On this site:* the chat demo's film-set strings (Rolling…, Take 1, Back to one) are exactly this register. The case studies have none. One light line per page is plenty.

9. **The title is a phrase, the subtitle is plain.** "Bottling Potions" over "small batch skincare branding and packaging". "Branding Anterior" over "Medical insurance?". The pair does the work a paragraph would. *On this site:* titles are rightly the business names, so the card one-liner is the subtitle. Right now five of six say "WordPress site for…". Make each one-liner the thing that is interesting about that project.

10. **They end warm and short.** On the people, or a plain statement, then credits. *On this site:* bodies end mid-inventory ("…and I've kept building on it since launch", "…and a tab for each space someone can rent"). End on the client using the thing.

## 2. The biggest problems across the site, ranked

**1. Every case study is the same sentence.**
Decks: "A website for DuctWorx, an Oklahoma City ductwork fabricator." / "A website for a casino, hotel, and RV park in the Oklahoma panhandle, built to…" / "A website for the organization behind Oklahoma City's innovation district, explaining…" / "The website for Koch Comm, the Oklahoma City marketing and advertising agency where I work, built to…" / "A redesign for Retirement Investment Advisors, a fee-only fiduciary firm in Oklahoma City, organizing…". Cards: "A modular WordPress site for…" / "A WordPress site for…" / "A WordPress site for…" / "The agency's own WordPress site…" / "A redesigned WordPress site…". Read in sequence on the Projects page, they blur into one project. Fix: open each on its own situation, and say WordPress once (or move the stack to a ledger row, see 7).

**2. Inventory, not story.**
Nobody wants anything, nothing is hard, and nobody decides. "Rooms and promotions are their own post types." "Events and news are post types the OKCID team publishes directly." "The library runs on custom post types with fields and tags in ACF." These are true and dull. The DuctWorx closer ("they've added projects, updated the roster, and rewritten copy without a single developer request") is the best line on the site because it is a thing that happened to people. Each page needs one of those.

**3. One claim, seven ways.**
"stays on brand without a developer" (DuctWorx deck), "keep the site current on its own" (DuctWorx body), "its team keeps current" (Golden Mesa card), "publishes directly" (OKCID body), "keep publishing on their own" (Koch Comm deck), "staff manage themselves" (RIA deck), "combines on their own" (RIA body). Self-serve is the real theme of the agency work, which is fine, but it has to be earned differently each time. DuctWorx owns "no developer request". Golden Mesa's version is "promotions drop off by themselves". RIA's is "the library files itself". Koch Comm's is "the writers publish without me". Give each page its own.

**4. Sentences that carry too much, and the colon stitch.**
The OKCID deck is one 38-word sentence. Koch Comm's is 36. Three bodies use the colon-to-list construction Michael dislikes: "and the site does the rest: it lands in the right archive…" (RIA), "the way they'd write a post: it appears on…" (Golden Mesa), "Try either version below: cue a line…" (chat app). Golden Mesa also has "three verbs, Play, Eat, and Stay, each opening…", a comma pile that makes the reader back up. All fixable with periods.

**5. Insider nouns with no hand-hold.**
"hot seat drawing", "custom post types", "ACF", "the comps", "one reducer drives both windows", "post types" (three pages). The audience is mixed: hiring managers, designers, clients, friends. Rule: either a plain phrase beside the term the first time, or cut it. Developer detail can live in the ledger or the questions-for-Michael answers, not the lede.

**6. The deck and the body say the same thing.**
DuctWorx: "a library of sections the team assembles… without a developer" (deck), then "built every section… without a single developer request" (body). OKCID: Innovation Hall gets "a tab for each room" in paragraph one and "a tab for each space someone can rent" in paragraph two. In a 120-word piece, repetition costs a third of the page.

**7. Corporate residue and filler.**
"stays on brand", "prospective client", "exactly the kind of campaign they're after", "designed and developed" (every meta description), "displayed on a laptop" (two card alts), "Selected websites and applications". None of it is terrible; all of it is the voice of an agency site, not a person's.

**8. Small text is inconsistent with itself.**
"All Projects" (title case) sits next to "Live site" (sentence case). The hidden headings say "Projects" then "Selected projects". The header has "Elsewhere" (nice) and "Other Stuff" (different register, and it holds one item). Art alt text is the title repeated. These are five-minute fixes and they are what a careful reader notices.

**One accessibility note that is really a writing note.** The project cards put `aria-label="View {{ cardTitle }} case study"` on the whole link, which replaces the card's own text as its accessible name. Screen-reader users never hear the one-liners. The same happens with `aria-label="View {{ title }}"` on the art buttons, which hides the image alt in the gallery (the dialog still reads it). Dropping those two aria-labels lets the real words through; the link text already says what it is.

## 3. Page by page

### Home (`src/_includes/home-content.njk`)

Approved, and it reads well. Two light flags, neither urgent.

> Hey there, I'm a developer, designer, and artist.
> I'm the senior front-end developer at Koch Comm in Oklahoma City. Outside of work, I make art, crochet, and sew, and I run cow-boys collab, an art business, with my best friend.
> Currently reading *Accelerando*

- Two consecutive sentences open "I'm". It is barely noticeable because the first is a greeting. Leave it unless the second paragraph gets reworked for another reason.
- "an art business" is the one abstract noun on the page. If cow-boys collab makes a nameable thing (prints, murals, shirts), say that instead and the line gets concrete: "and I run cow-boys collab, [prints and murals?], with my best friend." Question 1 below.

Home meta (`src/index.html`): "Portfolio of Michael Eggar, a front-end developer, designer, and artist based in Oklahoma City." Fine. Optional: "Michael Eggar, a front-end developer, designer, and artist in Oklahoma City. Websites, a film prop, and paintings." ("based in" is résumé language; "in" is how people talk.)

Social image alt (home and `site.json`): "DuctWorx website displayed across laptop and desktop screens" → **"The DuctWorx website on a laptop and a desktop monitor"**.

Showcase strings (`showcase.js`, `showcase-cards.njk`): "Explore projects and art", "View X case study", "View X on the Art page", and the status "{title}, Project 2026, 3 of 8" are all fine.

### DuctWorx (`src/projects/ductworx/index.html`)

**Title:** DuctWorx. Keep.

**Deck, current:**
> A website for DuctWorx, an Oklahoma City ductwork fabricator. I designed and built a library of sections the team assembles into pages, so the site stays on brand without a developer.

Problem: the second sentence is the body's first sentence again, and "stays on brand" is the client's marketing vocabulary, not Michael's.

The layout, type, and spacing come with it, so a page they build looks like one I built. `[What was the hardest section to make foolproof, and what did you do about it?]` Since launch they've added projects, updated the roster, and rewritten copy, and haven't needed to ask me once.

**Shorter alternative deck:** "A site for a ductwork fabricator, built from parts the office assembles itself."

**Meta description, current:** "A case study of a modular WordPress website designed and developed for DuctWorx, a ductwork fabricator in Oklahoma City."
→ **"DuctWorx, an Oklahoma City ductwork fabricator, wanted a site its office could update without a developer. Michael Eggar designed and built it in WordPress as a set of sections."**
